To Be Is to Make Oneself One:
Finite Unity as Made, and How to Test It
Abstract
Metaphysics offers two accounts of what makes a finite being one. On the first,
unity is given: a substrate persists and bears what changes. On the second, unity
is derived: a stability within a flux that traverses it. The first presupposes what
it means to explain; the second has no discriminant of individuated persistence.
This paper argues that both fail on the case that should be easiest for the first,
the stone. A finite determination is a difference held against a ground that, left
to itself, does not sustain it; every exposed finite being drains, and nothing real
escapes exposure. The stone therefore makes itself one, at a cost, and the pole
of the given loses its paradigmatic case. What remains is a third position, which
I state as an axiom: to be is to make oneself one. Unity is neither a state prior
to the act nor a stability abstracted from it, but that act insofar as it holds. The
axiom is testable. Perturb a finite being and locate where the irreversible trace of
its response falls: on a margin of its own, and it is aself; on an external bearer’s,
and it is acarriage; on none, and it is anaggregate. Every finite being makes
itself one; only a self remakes itself. I argue finally that for a being grounded on
a finite margin this making is not optional, since inaction is not a stable state
but dissolution.
Keywords:individuation; finite unity; substance; process; irreversibility; meta-
physics of persistence
1 Two Accounts, and What Both Concede
1.1 The question
What makes a being one being? Not what there is, nor what kinds of thing the world
contains, but under what conditions something holds as one individual rather than
as a coordinated plurality, an effect of conceptual carving, or a stability of use. The
question is classical without being exhausted, and its persistence is not accidental:
1
most answers presuppose what they mean to explain. To say that an entity isthis
entity, one needs a principle that individuates it; but if that principle is an essence,
a form, or a sortal, it demands in turn to be individuated. To say that it persists
through change, one must designate it as the same before and after, which presupposes
the criterion of identity being sought.
Two families of answer occupy the field, and they are usually presented as rivals. I
want to show that they concede the same thing.
1.2 Unity as given
On the first account, unity is prior to activity. Aristotle gives the position its lasting
form: primary substance, what is neither said of a subject nor in a subject, is the
first candidate for unity, andthisSocrates exists independently of the qualities he
bears (Categories;MetaphysicsZ). The gesture prevents unity from dissolving into a
multiplicity of predicates. But positing that there are primary substances does not say
what makes a being hold as this subject. Substance names unity without exhibiting
its operation.
Leibniz reasserts singularity in another idiom, at the cost of commitments that
makeindividuationdependonpostulatesexternaltoit: completeconcepts, pre-established
harmony, a theory of simplicity. The criterion is operative in the logical sense rather
than the empirical one. It does not indicate how to test, on a concrete configuration,
whether there is one. Strawson (1959) inherits the problem without the theologi-
cal resources and reformulates it descriptively, asking how we identify and re-identify
particulars within a shared spatio-temporal framework. The answer is precise but
epistemically oriented; the ontological question is defused by translation rather than
resolved. One can know how we identify an individual without knowing what makes
it one.
Contemporary substance ontologies retain the essential commitment. Wiggins
(2001) makes identity through change turn on the sortal concept under which a thing
falls, so that what a thing is fixes the conditions under which it remains that thing.
Lowe (1998, 2006) grounds persistence in the possession of dispositions that need not
be exercised: what persists does so by what itwoulddo, not by what it does. Koslicki
(2018)revivesahylomorphicanswerinwhichformistheprinciplebywhichasubstance
is one, which meets the presuppositional worry head on and answers it by taking form
as an explanatorily prior constituent. Whatever else divides these positions, they agree
that there is something the finite being is, beneath or before what it does.

1.3 Unity as derived
On the second account, unity is posterior to activity. The individual is a temporary
crystallization within a becoming (Rescher 1996); organisms are processes actively
maintained at several timescales (Nicholson and Dupré 2018); persistence is the per-
sistence of processes rather than of things (Meincke and Dupré 2020). The gain is
real. The inert substrate is abandoned, and change ceases to be a problem requiring
a bearer.
The loss is a discriminant. If unity is a stability in a flux, then any sufficiently
stable pattern qualifies, and the account owes an answer to the question of which
stabilities individuate. A whirlpool maintains a form through a complete turnover of
its matter; so does a cell. Under a criterion of processual persistence they are on a
footing. Processualism can describe the difference between them but cannot derive
it, because the resource that would derive it, something belonging to one and not the
other, is what the account has given up.
I leave the pair here and return to it in §5. The test proposed there is directed at
this pole as much as at the first, and the whirlpool is where it does its work: what
separates it from the cell is neither a degree of stability nor of renewal, and the criterion
has to say what it is.
1.4 The shared concession
The two positions look symmetrical, and their symmetry is the point. Both distribute
being and doing across two planes; they differ only over which plane comes first. The
substantialist places identity before activity: there is a thing, and then operations that
conserve it. The processualist places activity before identity: there is a flux, and then
stabilities within it. Neither considers that the distribution is itself the error.
This is not a merely formal observation, because the distribution is what produces
both failures. If there is first a thing and then its operations, the thing must be
individuated independently, and the presupposition returns. If there is first activity
and then its stabilities, nothing in the activity marks one stability off from another,
and the discriminant is missing. Remove the distribution and both failures lose their
source.
1.5 Two senses of “one”
One distinction must be announced before the argument begins, because without it
§2 will be misread. There are two senses of “one”, and they do not coincide. In a
minimal sense, every finite being makes itself one: it holds as a determination, under
3
constraint and at a cost. In a strong sense, only some beingsremakethemselves one,
regenerating their own conditions through a cycle of their own, whereas most made
unities come undone as soon as they are perturbed. The stone makes itself one and
does not remake itself; the organism does both.
The argument of §2 bears on the first sense, and establishes it universally. The
test of §5 bears on the second, and discriminates. Nothing in §2 is therefore a claim
that the stone and the organism hold as one in the same way. The question of what
separates them is deferred, not levelled.
A note on method.The continental tradition has reached a version of the first diag-
nosis. Simondon (2005) argues that any ontology setting out from the constituted
individual to seek its principle presupposes what it means to explain, and that individ-
uation must be thought from a pre-individual field. The diagnosis is just, and I take it
as independent confirmation. But it remains a diagnosis: transductive individuation
names the structure classical ontologies lack without furnishing a procedure that de-
cides a concrete case. My principal interlocutor is therefore the analytic literature, for
its discipline of explicit criteria and limit-cases.
2 The Stone
2.1 Why this case
The argument of this section is directed at the first pole, and it is conducted on the
terrain that pole would choose. The case against inert givenness is easy to make
with organisms, and worthless for that reason: no one holds that a bacterium is a
substrate on which properties are imprinted. The substantialist’s strongest instance
is the apparently inert, closed, propertied thing, the stone or the rock that has lain in
the same place for ten thousand years and appears to require nothing of itself in order
to remain what it is. If the stone turns out to be a made unity, the pole of the given
does not lose a peripheral example. It loses its paradigm.
2.2 A boundary is a boundary only maintained
Begin with what it is to be finite. A finite determination is a difference held against
a ground that, left to itself, does not sustain it. To be finite is to be bounded, to be
thisrather than the surrounding indistinction. But a bound does not posit itself; a
boundary is a boundary only insofar as it is maintained, and the surroundings have
no reason to respect a line they do not draw. An unsustained difference is therefore
perpetually to be renewed, or it fades.
4
This much is conceptual, and true a priori of anything finite. It is legible on
finitude alone and requires no physics. Call it thetask: the standing requirement that
a bounded difference in grip on a ground be renewed in order to remain that difference.
2.3 Two magnitudes under one word
The task is not yet a cost, and the two must be separated, because the substantialist’s
best reply exploits their conflation.
Under the word “cost” hide two magnitudes. The first is modal and static: what it
would take to undo the determination, which for a crystal is the depth of its potential
well. This magnitude is a priori in the sense just given, being the task read as a
quantity. The second is dynamic: maintaining the determinationexpendssomething
each cycle, so that a drain is levied and not recovered. This second magnitude is not
read a priori. A perfectly reversible world would know the same task without the
least drain, since differences would still have to be held and holding them would cost
nothing.
That holding drains follows from our world being irreversible, of which the second
lawisoneexpression. Thisisapositabouttheworldratherthanaconceptualnecessity,
and I mark it as such: it is judged downstream, by what it lets one see, not on its
intrinsic evidence.
2.4 The crystal, and why it does not save the given
The distinction just drawn gives the substantialist his sharpest objection, and I state it
at full strength. Take a perfect crystal at absolute zero. The static cost remains, since
it would still take work to undo it, but the drain is nil. At equilibrium, nothing further
is expended. Here the two magnitudes divorce, and here the substantialist appears to
recover his inert given: a finite being that is, without having to do anything at all.
He does not recover it, for two reasons, and the second is the one that matters.
The first is that the crystal does not exist. Absolute zero is inaccessible; every real
crystal is at finite temperature, hence agitated, hence draining by diffusion, creep, and
sublimation, however infinitesimally. And abstracting from the thermal, no structure
escapes, at cosmological timescales, the background radiation traversing it or the slow
decay of its matter. Where the thermal drain would vanish, another nameable mech-
anism restores it. Zero drain is the never-attained limit of a series of beings that all
drain.
The second reason is that the ideal crystal is not merely unrealized but incoherent
as described. To be finite and to be exposed are one property rather than two. A
finite is a difference bounded against a ground, hence in grip on that ground, hence
5
exposed to it. A finite with no grip on any ground would be bounded against nothing,
which is to say it would be the Whole, or a pure possible, and not a finite. The
ideal crystal is constructed by removing exposure while retaining finitude, and that
operation cannot be carried out. It is not a counterexample to the claim but a non-
instantiated idealization obtained by deleting a constituent of the very concept it
instantiates.
The anti-substantialist claim is thereby made precise rather than lost. It is not
that everything is dynamic in a sense the ideal crystal would falsify. It is that every
exposed finite being drains, and that nothing real escapes exposure.
2.5 What is established, and what is not
Established: no real finite being is inertly given. The stone is not a block of being
laid down once and for all, but a determination in grip on a ground that wears it, and
therefore indexed to time and to a milieu rather than indifferent to both. Its unity is
made. The pole of the given has lost the case it would have chosen.
An equivocation must be refused here, because the word “cost” carries two magni-
tudes that the argument must not be allowed to trade on. For a being with a cycle,
expenditure buys continuation: the reserves drawn down purchase the next round. For
the stone, nothing of the sort occurs. Its bonds sit in a potential well and are not re-
newed by any work, and the drain it undergoes is not a price paid for holding but the
erosion of what holds. A substantialist is entitled to press exactly this: the depth of
the well is granted, and the dynamic drain measures the stone’s failure to hold rather
than its holding.
I grant the point and narrow the claim accordingly. What §2 requires is not that
the stone pays for its unity, but that no real finite being is exempt from exposure. The
inert given demands a determination indifferent to the ground it is bounded against,
and there is none: exposure is not an accident of the stone but a constituent of finitude.
That is what “makes itself one” means in the minimal sense of §1.5, and it means no
more than that. Whether anything buys its own continuation is a further question,
which §5 answers and §2 does not.
Not established, and the debt should be exact. That the non-sustaining isdirected,
that an unrenewed finite declines rather than reinforces itself, is taken here as a struc-
tural trait of the irreversible world and not derived from anything more primitive. Its
ultimate reduction to a combinatorial or thermodynamic condition, and the treatment
of recurrence over long timescales, exceed this paper. The argument of §2 therefore
has two grades: the task is conceptual, the drain is posited, and the conclusion inherits
the weaker of the two.
6
What §2 establishes belongs to the minimal sense of §1.5. That the stone does not
remakeitself is a further fact, which §5 turns into a test.
The two poles are now both retired: the given has lost its paradigm here, and the
derived was shown in §1.3 to lack a discriminant. What remains is not a compromise
between them but the position they jointly exclude.
3 The Position That Remains
3.1 A dichotomy, not a survey
Both available accounts distribute being and doing across two planes and differ only
over which comes first. The one that places identity first has now lost the case it would
itself have chosen; the one that places activity first cannot say which continuities of
activity are the activity of one being.
What follows is not that some third position has been proved. It is narrower and
more useful. The disjunctioneither being and doing are distributed across two planes,
or they are notis exhaustive, and both orderings of the distribution have now been
shown to fail, each for a reason internal to it. Whatever survives must therefore deny
the distribution itself. That is a constraint on the space of remaining positions, not
yet an occupant of it.
The axiom is the occupant I propose:
To be is to make oneself one.
For every finite being, unity is not a state prior to the act but the act itself insofar
as it holds. Not, on one side, a thing that is one, and on the other the operations
that maintain it: one plane, an act whose unity is the continuous enactment of its own
holding.
I want to be exact about what the preceding sections license. They license the
denial of the distribution. They do not license this particular way of denying it, which
is a substantive proposal and could be wrong while the diagnosis stands. What the
axiom must now do is earn the difference between itself and a mere negation. It must
have content, and that content must make a difference to cases. §4 states the content;
§5 puts it to a test that it can fail.
3.2 Why each term is load-bearing
Every word in the formulation is doing work, and the quickest way to show it is
to remove them one at a time.To benames finite actuality, not existence in the
7
unrestricted logical sense; the axiom is a claim about finite determinations, and I take
no position on whether anything else falls under it.To makemarks constitution rather
than fabrication, and nothing in it implies an agent who first exists and then produces
itself, a reading treated in §3.3.Oneselfmarks that the act and what is constituted in
it are not two independently individuated terms.Onemarks that the act is not sheer
occurrence but holds as a determination.
Now remove them. “To be is to act” describes a world of activity in which nothing
individuates, and the question of §1 returns unchanged: why call some regions of this
activity beings? “To be is to persist” reinstates a bearer given before what it does, and
§2 has removed its best case. “To be is to make oneself” is generalized self-production,
processualism with a reflexive verb, and it loses the constraint that would separate the
cell from the storm.
The operative word is thereforeone. What it does not mean must be said. It does
not mean numerical simplicity: an organism has trillions of components and is one in
the relevant sense. It does not mean indivisibility, nor impermeability, nor the absence
of nested cycles. It means that the act holds as a determination, so that changes can
be attributed to the same finite unity rather than merely redescribed as successive
states of an unrestricted field.
This is why I speak of unity-in-act. Unity is neither deposited beneath the activity
nor extracted from it afterwards. The act is one insofar as it holds, and the being is
that holding insofar as it occurs.
3.3 “Oneself” without a prior self
An objection threatens the axiom before it is deployed, and it bears on two words. The
reflexive “oneself” appears to posit a subject that makes and an object on which the
making falls, hence a subject prior to the act, which is what the axiom refuses. And
“one” redoubles the threat: to make one, is this not for a unifier to gather a unified,
reintroducing beneath unity the duality of what unifies and what is unified? Under
this double reading the axiom reinstates twice the division it means to lift.
The objection reads “oneself” as reflexive. The construction I mean is middle,
in the technical sense of comparative grammar. Benveniste (1966) characterizes the
middle voice as designating processes in which the subject is itself engaged in what
it accomplishes rather than being the external agent of an action falling on a distinct
patient:gignomai, to be born,phainomai, to appear. The subject is the site where
the process takes place, not a term standing outside it.
Read in that voice, both threats dissolve. The subject is not given before the act,
since it is in the act as its site; and “one” does not presuppose a unifier distinct from the
8
unified, since unification is the mode under which the act holds and not an operation
performed on a previously multiple matter. To make oneself one, in the middle voice,
is to hold as one without the holding and the held coming apart.
Grammar does not settle ontology, and the middle voice does not show that unity
so conceived is real rather than verbal. Its role is narrower and sufficient: the sentence
need not carry the circularity a reflexive reading imposes on it, so the objection does
not dispose of the axiom before the argument begins. The burden falls on the test of
§5, which the axiom can fail.
One restriction, which will matter later. At the level of the axiom, “oneself” names
no interiority, no agency, no intention, and no self in any strong sense; it marks only
the non-externality of a finite being to its own enactment. That every finite being
makes itself one is what the axiom says. That some finite beings are selves is not, and
requires the further structure of remaking.
3.4 How an axiom is to be judged
Calling this an axiom is not a way of exempting it from argument, and I should be
explicit about the standard I am accepting. An axiom is an explicit primitive, and the
question to put to it is not whether it is self-evident, since it is not, and neither is any
of its rivals. The question is whether it discriminates cases its rivals leave undecided, at
a cost in commitments one can state. I have stated the commitments in §3.2 and will
add the rest in §4. The discrimination is §5’s business, and I have built that section
so that it can come out the wrong way.
What follows immediately is the content. The axiom compresses three claims that
it mobilizes jointly and that are not derivable from one another. Taken separately,
each falls back into a position §1 has already removed.
4 What the Axiom Commits One To
The axiom compresses three claims. They are mobilized jointly, they are not derivable
from one another, and their solidarity is what makes the displacement. I state them
here because §5 depends on all three, and because a reader is entitled to see the bill
before the test.
4.1 Constitutive activity
Nothing finite is inertly given. Every finite being is bounded against a ground that
wears it, and to cease absolutely to make itself is to cease to be. This is not the claim
that everything transforms visibly, repairs itself actively, or buys its continuation with
9
work; it is the claim, narrowed in §2.5, that no finite determination is indifferent to
what it is bounded against. What it excludes is the finite being as an inert substrate
on which properties are imprinted. It is the weakest of the three, and the one for which
§2 supplied an independent argument.
4.2 The identity of being and doing
Thesecondisstronger, anditdoesnotfollowfromthefirst. Thatafinitedetermination
presupposes constitutive activity does not yet forbid holding that there is first a thing
and then operations that conserve it, a bearer that acts rather than a bearer given
inertly. The axiom refuses this residual distribution as well.
Doing is not an attribute added to being. It is its modality. Correlatively, being is
not a silent support beneath doing; it is that doing insofar as it holds as one. This is
the content that separates the axiom from substantialism, which gives the substrate
as primary, and from radical processualism, which gives the flux as primary without
stabilizing what in it would be. The middle voice (§3.3) is the grammatical resource
that makes the identification statable without circularity; it is not what makes it true.
This content is the one §5 leans on hardest, and the reason belongs here. If being
and doing were distinct, a thing on one side and its maintenance on the other, then
the question of whose margin bears the cost would be a question about where an
accountant draws a line. One could redescribe the whirlpool as a being whose mainte-
nance operations happen to be performed by the river, and the organism as one whose
operations happen to be performed internally, with no ontological difference between
them, only a difference in the boundary chosen for the audit. The criterion would then
record a convention.
Under the identity, that redescription is unavailable. The whirlpool is not a thing
that has its holding paid for elsewhere. Itisthe holding, and if the holding is the
river’s doing, there is no second being there that borrows. The difference between
bearing one’s own cost and having it borne is therefore not a fact about where an
external boundary is drawn, but about whether the act traces a boundary at all. This
is what will make the verdicts of §5 more than a bookkeeping choice, and it is the
point at which the axiom stops being an ornament on the criterion and becomes its
condition.
4.3 The monism of the act
If the finite being is never other than the doing by which it holds as one, there are
not two orders of reality, an inert ground and a dynamic regime, a matter and a mind,
to be articulated afterwards. There is a single category of which all finite beings are
10
modalizations.
This is not a reductionism. It does not say that all beings resemble one another, but
that their differences must be thought as differences of regime within one type of being
rather than as differences between two irreducible orders. Without it, the trichotomy
of §5 would be three analogies rather than one criterion: the stone, the organism, and
the whirlpool would be sorted by three unrelated standards, and the claim that a
single question discriminates them would have no ground. With it, the same question
is well-posed across the range, and the answers differ because the regimes differ, not
because the beings belong to different worlds.
Monism is also what keeps the axiom from being a biological criterion in disguise.
If self-maintenance marked a separate order of being, “to be is to make oneself one”
would be a thesis about living things with an unexplained remainder for everything
else. It is instead a thesis about finite determination as such, of which self-maintenance
is one regime.
4.4 Why the three hold together
The solidarity can be checked by removing each in turn. Activity without the identity
of being and doing makes activity a superadded attribute, and a substrate reappears
beneath it. Identity without monism produces a dualism in which being-doing holds
of one category and something else holds of the rest, and the criterion loses its range.
Monismwithoutactivityproducesindistinctstaticentities, andindividuationcollapses
into the question of how to carve one homogeneous stuff. Together the three draw one
configuration: a finite being exclusively, non-dually, and costingly in act, without a
reserve of inertia and without a division of planes.
One consequence must be recorded, because it is what the test will exploit. If
every finite being makes itself one, no finite being is exempt from the question of
where the cost of its holding falls. The question is universal in application and, this is
the substantive part, not universally answerable in the same way: the drain is real for
every exposed finite being, but a cycle that takes the drain in charge is not. Whether
anything answers to “who pays” is what differs across cases, and it is the differing that
§5 reads.
5 The Test
5.1 What renewal does not see
Take the two cases left standing in §1.3. An organism and a whirlpool both present
a stable form traversed by a complete turnover of their matter. Both persist through
11
the replacement of their components; both would be described, in the vocabulary
of processual persistence, as organizations actively maintained across time. Under
a criterion of renewal alone they are indiscernible, and a theory bounded to such
a criterion must either class them together or decide between them by appeal to
something it has not stated.1
The form of this objection needs care. It is not that processual accounts are unable
to notice the difference. They notice it, and describe it well. It is that the resource
which wouldderivethe difference is the one such accounts have given up. If unity is a
stability within a flux, then what individuates a stability can only be a feature of the
stability: its degree, its duration, its complexity, its causal integration. None of these
separates the cases. The whirlpool is stable, persists indefinitely under a maintained
flow, has an intricate internal structure, and is causally integrated in the strong sense
that every part of it depends on every other. What it lacks is not a further degree of
anything on that list.
Rescher (1996) posits the individual as a temporary crystallization within becom-
ing; Nicholson and Dupré (2018) treat organisms as processes actively maintained at
several timescales; Meincke and Dupré (2020) locate persistence in processes rather
than things. Each is a defensible account of how identity survives change, and none of
them, on its own resources, determines which of two equally maintained organizations
is the one being. That is not a failure of care but a consequence of having placed
activity before unity, which is the second horn of the distribution §1.4 identified.
5.2 The test
What is not visible under renewal is visible under perturbation.
By a perturbation I mean any event that displaces a finite being from its nominal
regime and forces it to mobilize whatever capacity it has to respond: a thermal shock
for a cell, a material failure for an artifact, a supply breakdown for an organization.
What makes such an event a test is not its physical nature but its structure. The
response it demands leaves a trace that is not restored when the perturbation ceases.
The cell that has synthesized its heat-shock proteins does not recover the energy spent.
The wound closes as a scar and not as the prior tissue. Returning from B to A is not
the erasure of the passage from A to B, but a further transformation with a cost of its

One restriction, and it is a restriction rather than a promise. What follows is a

unit, no threshold, and no case in which the measurement has been carried out, and
nothing in the argument turns on my doing so. Specifying an observable that satisfies
the criterion in a given system is a separate task, and a harder one than stating the
criterion. What the test does presuppose is the drain posited in §2.3: if that posit
fails there is no trace to locate, and this section fails with it. Beyond that it assumes
nothing about which beings have margins, which is what it is built to find out.
5.3 Three answers, and two privations
Three answers to that question exhaust the field, and the exhaustiveness is structural
rather than enumerative (Figure 1). Either some cycle takes the trace in charge, or
none does. If none does, the trace disperses and accumulates. If some cycle does,
either it draws on a resource of its own or it draws on another’s. Where the cost falls
on a margin proper to the being, the being is aself. Where it is borne by an external
infrastructure or bearer, the being is acarriage. Where no cycle takes it up at all, the
being is anaggregate.
Carriage and aggregate are not two further positive kinds standing alongside the
first. They are two privations. The carriage has the cycle and lacks the endorsement;
the aggregate lacks the cycle, hence lacks anywhothat could pay. This asymmetry
disposes of a circularity that would otherwise threaten the question itself. “Who pays?”
has a determinate answer only where a cycle traces one. For a carriage the answer
is traced by an observer who decides where to draw the audit; for an aggregate the
question is without object, since an aggregate is not a unity for which one must decide
who pays. To ask who pays, of an aggregate, is already to presuppose in it the self it
lacks.

5.5 Composition, and the life answer
The verdicts bear on a question analytic metaphysics has posed in another form. Un-
der what conditions do several things compose one thing? Answers range from the
universalism that lets any plurality compose (Sider 2001) to restrictions of various
strengths, with conservative defences of the ordinary furniture in between (Korman
2015). The framing treats unity as a relation between a plurality and an object.
Van Inwagen (1990) gives the answer closest to the present one, and the proximity
is instructive. Composition occurs, on his account, when the activity of the simples
constitutes a life. This makes an activity rather than a relation the condition of
composite unity, which is the same displacement I am proposing. The divergence is
over what makes an activity constitutive. In van Inwagen’s account, life is where the
answer stops: asked why a life composes and a maintained vortex does not, one can
point to the biological and no further. The endorsement criterion says what the appeal
to life was standing in for, namely that the activity bears on its own margin the cost
of its own resumption, and it separates the organism from the whirlpool by a property
neither of them wears as a kind term.
The gain is that the criterion is structural rather than sortal. Two distant grains
of sand compose nothing, for want of a common endorsement, even where an arbitrary
mereological fusion unites them; and an entity may fall outside the biological and still
endorse, or fall inside it and not. A full engagement with restrictivism, universalism,
and priority monism requires a separate treatment. What I retain is that “who pays?”
discriminates where “which pluralities compose?” remains indifferent.
5.6 Why “its own margin” is not circular
The obvious objection is that the criterion smuggles in what it was meant to produce:
to say that the resourcebelongs tothe being is to have given oneself the being in
advance, since the boundary that decides whose margin it is, is the boundary the
criterion was supposed to trace.
The objection bites if ownership is read as possession, and read that way the
criterion fails at once, since the organism too depends on what it does not make.
It dissipates entropy into its milieu, imports amino acids and cofactors it does not
synthesize, and would die within minutes of isolation. Autarky cannot be the criterion,
16
because dependence on a milieu is universal and discriminates nothing.
What is required is a property of the cycle itself, statable without a possessor and
without a prior boundary: the margin is proper when the effects of a reconfiguration
re-enter, within the being, into the conditions of the next cycle. The heat-shock protein
the cell has synthesized modifies the state that conditions its subsequent metabolism,
andtheloopclosesinside. Inthewhirlpool, therearrangementofthefluidexitsintothe
river and conditions no cycle of its own; in the flame, the consumed wax conditions
the next moment of burning but is not among the things the flame makes. Which
effects re-enter which conditions is a mereological property of the cause-effect chain,
requiring no pilot, no possessor, and no boundary drawn in advance. The circularity
falls there.
I borrow the property rather than construct it. It has been developed under the
namesofoperationalandorganizationalclosurebyMaturanaandVarela(1980), Varela
(1979), Rosen (1991), Montévil and Mossio (2015), and Moreno and Mossio (2015).3 I
take it here only in the role the objection requires, as what makes “its own” statable
non-circularly, and not as a rival criterion of individuation.
This is also where §4.2 is discharged. Under the identity of being and doing, bearing
one’s own cost rather than having it borne is not a fact about where an observer draws
the audit. The whirlpool is one only for an observer who traces the boundary of who
pays; the organism traces that boundary itself, since its beingisthat holding and the
holding is what closes. Strong individuation is the existence of an answer to “who
pays?” traced by the structure rather than by the observer, which is what an endorsed
closed cycle furnishes and what both privations lack.

margin. This is why the cost is not a tracer of organization, and why §5.6 borrowed

is divided. That is why I hold that it does not vary with the observer’s carving; but
the claim is empirical in form, and it is the place where the account is most exposed.
6 Is Any of This Necessary?
One could grant everything in §5 and still hold that it describes a contingency. Beings
in our world happen to have margins; some cycles happen to be arranged so that their
effects re-enter their own conditions; the criterion sorts what it finds. On this reading
the axiom would be a summary of local furniture, and “to be is to make oneself one”
would be a fact about how things have turned out rather than a claim about finite
being. This section argues that the reading is unavailable, and it argues it in a way
that can be checked, by constructing the being for which the necessity would not hold
and showing what it costs to be that being.

6.1 The reductio
Suppose the identity of being and doing were contingent, so that it were conceivable
for a finite being to be without doing, or for a doing to take place without being. There
are two cases, and each ruins the axiom for a reason already established.
A being without doing reinstates the inert substrate. There would be finite being
inertly given, which §4.1 excludes and §2 argued against on the case least favorable to
it. Every finite determination holds under constraint, is to be renewed, and to cease
making itself is to cease to be.
A doing without being introduces a flux with no bearer. There would be two orders
of reality, pure activity on one side and being on the other, which §4.3 excludes, since
there is a single category of which all finite beings are modalizations.
The identity of being and doing is therefore not an exchangeable stipulation. Its
negation is not false by observation; it is incoherent with the contents the axiom
mobilizes. But this establishes less than it appears to, and §6.2 says why.
6.2 The separating model
The reductio invites a deflationary reply, and the reply is a good one. If “to cease
acting is to cease to be” follows from the way the terms have been set up, then the
necessity is verbal: one has defined a being as an act and then discovered that an act
which stops has stopped. Nothing about the world has been learned.
The reply is answered by constructing the alternative, and the alternative is coher-
ent.
Consider two acts.5 The first is self-grounded. It has no external ground, its
capacity to act is endogenous, and the margin on which its holding is paid is its own.
For this act, not paying the cost is exhausting itself, and inaction is not a stable option
but dissolution. The second is contingent in a precise sense: it has an external ground
that compensates its cost. What becomes optional for it is not renewal but thepaying
of renewal, since an external source discharges the drain in its place, so that waste
does not threaten its existence. It still makes itself, like every finite being. It simply
does not pay what it costs.
The difference between the two is structural rather than verbal, and the second is
constructible without contradiction. What renders the act necessary is therefore not

6.3 Neither logical, nor external, nor contingent
The position can now be located among the terms modal metaphysics has fixed.
It is not logical necessity. “Making oneself one” is not a tautology, and its negation
is not a formal contradiction but an incoherence with the axiom’s contents. It is not
causal or external necessity, since no transcendent instance imposes it and no external
law grounds it. It is immanent, holding to the structure of what it characterizes.6
The two magnitudes of §2.3 reappear here as two necessities, and conflating them
would overstate the result. The necessity of thetaskdoes not pass through cost: even
in a perfectly reversible world the necessity of making oneself would remain entire, and
only its price would vanish. That strand is a priori and thin, being near-analytic given
the axiom. The necessity with discriminating content is the one §6.2 exhibits: it runs
on cost and is posited rather than derived.
I state the result at the strength it has. Immanent necessity is thin on both sides
of the joint, analytic upstream and posited downstream, and its value is not a modal
discovery. Its value is that it blocks the deflationary reading with which this section
opened. Endorsement is not a contingent regularity that our world happens to exhibit,
but what it is for something to hold on a finite margin without an external ground.

7 Scope
7.1 What is established
The argument has been deliberately narrow: an axiom, a criterion, and a relation
between them. The criterion is that a finite being holds as one in the strong sense if
and only if it bears, on a margin of its own, the cost of regenerating its own conditions,
which is testable by locating where the irreversible trace of a perturbation falls and non-
circularbecause“itsown” isapropertyofthecausalgraphratherthanofpossession. Of
the three, the relation is what I would defend first. Without the identity of being and
doing, endorsement records where an observer draws an audit; with it, the difference
between paying and being paid for is a difference in whether the act traces a boundary
at all. That is what §4.2 secured and §5.6 discharged, and it is why the criterion is
not detachable from the axiom that produced it.
7.2 Three grades of verdict
The criterion does not decide uniformly, and this is a property of the criterion rather
than an embarrassment to be managed.
Some verdicts are determinate. The stone is an aggregate, the organism a self, the
whirlpool a carriage. The endorsement structure is unambiguous, and the verdict is
stable under reformulation.
Some are conditional: a dune renewed by the wind, a colony whose cycle may close
at a level above its members. Here the criterion does not deliver a category but a
decision procedure, and the verdict turns on parameters that can be identified and
measured.
Some cases are indeterminate, and the criterion records the indeterminacy as a
22
result. Where endorsement is stratified or partly externalized, the conditions of appli-
cation are not jointly satisfied, and there is no fact of the matter that the criterion is
failing to detect.
Rival accounts decide uniformly, whether by a biological criterion, a mereological
one, or a derivation from the whole, and thereby treat structurally ambiguous cases
as though the ambiguity were a defect of the theory. I take it to be a property of the
phenomenon, and prefer a criterion that says where it decides, where it conditions,
and where it suspends.
7.3 What is not established
Four debts, stated rather than deferred.
The drain is posited rather than derived (§2.3). That an unrenewed finite declines
rather than reinforces itself is taken as a structural trait of the irreversible world;
its reduction to a combinatorial or thermodynamic condition, and the treatment of
recurrence over long timescales, exceed this paper. The conclusions that depend on it
inherit its grade.
That finitude entails exposure (§2.4) is a posit about the connectedness of the
world, not a theorem. A plurality of causally disjoint finite islands is not obviously
incoherent, and I have argued only that an island bounded against nothing would not
be a finite; I have not shown that no other arrangement is available. The step is
load-bearing for §2 and it is asserted.
The observer-independence of the trace’s location (§5.8) is empirical in form. I
have argued that what re-enters what is not a matter of choice, and this is where the
account is most exposed.
The necessity of §6 is thin, and I have said so where it is stated rather than in a
concluding qualification.
7.4 Final reformulation
The unity of a finite being is neither the instantiation of a substance nor the crys-
tallization of a process. It is the regime in which an act holds as one by remaking
itself, under the constraint of having to endorse its own cost. At this level, and only at
this level, does the question “what is a being?” stop opposing what remains to what
becomes, and become a more exact question, addressable in cases: what, in the finite,
holds enough to pay for itself the price of its own holding?
